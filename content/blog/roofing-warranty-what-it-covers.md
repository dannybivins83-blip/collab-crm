"Fifty-year warranty" is one of the most reassuring things a roofing salesperson can say and one of the least precise. There are two separate warranties on a new roof, they cover different failures, and the impressive number usually refers to the one that does not cover the thing most likely to go wrong.

This is not a reason to distrust anybody. It is a reason to ask two specific questions before you sign and to collect two specific documents after the job.

## There are two warranties, not one

**The manufacturer warranty** covers the shingle. If the product itself fails — granule loss outside spec, premature cracking, a manufacturing defect — this is the one that responds. It comes from the manufacturer, not the contractor, and it survives the contractor going out of business.

**The workmanship warranty** covers the installation. If the roof leaks because a flashing was set wrong, a valley was cut badly, or a pipe boot was not sealed, the shingle is not defective. The installation is. That is the contractor's warranty, and it is a separate promise.

Here is why the distinction matters: **most roof leaks are installation, not product.** The warranty that is most likely to be the one you need is the one that depends entirely on the contractor still being in business and still honoring it — and it is the one some quotes never mention at all.

| | Manufacturer warranty | Workmanship warranty |
|---|---|---|
| Covers | The shingle itself | How it was installed |
| Comes from | The manufacturer | The contractor |
| Typical length | 25 years to "lifetime", with conditions | 1 to 10+ years, entirely up to the contractor |
| Survives contractor closing | Yes | No |
| Covers a leak at a wall or chimney | No | Yes |

## The first way it quietly isn't valid: you got the basic one

Manufacturers usually offer a tiered system. The basic warranty applies automatically to anyone who buys the shingle. The enhanced warranties — the long numbers, the ones that also cover labor to fix a defect, the ones that are transferable more than once — generally require two things:

1. The roof was installed by a contractor the manufacturer has certified, and
2. The warranty was **registered** with the manufacturer after the job, usually within a set window.

Miss either and you have the basic warranty. Nobody tells you this. There is no letter. You find out years later when you file a claim.

The enhanced versions also typically require a full system — the manufacturer's own starter strip, ridge cap, underlayment and ventilation, not a mix of products. This is exactly why "are you using the manufacturer's starter strip, or cut field shingles?" is a warranty question and not a nitpick.

**Ask before you sign:** *"Which specific manufacturer warranty do I get, who registers it, and what do I need to do to keep it valid?"*

A real answer names the warranty, says the contractor registers it, and mentions the deadline. A vague answer about "fifty years" is not an answer.

## The second way: ventilation

Inadequate attic ventilation is one of the most common reasons a manufacturer declines a claim. Heat and moisture build up under the deck, the shingles age from underneath, and the manufacturer's position is that the product was not installed into a system it was warranted for.

The part that catches people out is that ventilation is a two-part system. Exhaust at the ridge does nothing without enough intake at the soffits to feed it. A contractor can add a ridge vent, show you the new vent, and leave you with a roof that still cannot breathe because the soffits are blocked or undersized.

**Ask before you sign:** *"What ventilation am I getting, and is there enough intake at the soffits to feed it?"*

## What to ask about the workmanship warranty

Three things, and they are all short:

- **How many years?** This varies enormously between contractors and is one of the few places a higher quote is visibly buying you something.
- **Is it in writing?** A verbal warranty is not a warranty. It needs to be in the contract or on company letterhead.
- **Does it transfer if I sell the house?** A transferable warranty is worth real money at closing. Many are not transferable, and some transfer once.

Also worth asking plainly: how long has the company been operating under this name? A ten-year workmanship warranty from a company in its first year is a ten-year promise from an entity that statistically may not be there. That is not an accusation, it is just arithmetic, and it is a fair thing to weigh.

## What to collect when the job is done

Do not let the final payment go before you have:

1. **The registered manufacturer warranty certificate** — the actual document, emailed to you, with your address and the correct product on it. Not a promise to register it.
2. **The written workmanship warranty** — years stated, signed, on letterhead.
3. **The closed permit** — the county's record that the work passed final inspection.
4. **The final invoice marked paid in full** — your protection against a lien from a supplier or subcontractor the contractor did not pay.

Those four documents are what you will want in ten years, and they are much harder to collect after the final check has cleared than before.

## The short version

Ask which manufacturer warranty you actually get and who registers it. Ask how many years the workmanship warranty runs, whether it is in writing, and whether it transfers. Then collect the four documents above before the last payment.

That is roughly ten minutes of effort, and it is the difference between having a warranty and believing you have one.
