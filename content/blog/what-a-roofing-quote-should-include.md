Most residential roofing quotes are one page: a company name, a short paragraph, and a number. That is not enough to agree on, and it is not enough to compare. The work below is what a re-roof actually consists of, and every line is something a quote can either commit to or stay quiet about.

Use this as a checklist against the quotes you already have. The useful question for each line is not "did they do a good job explaining this" — it is simply **is it on the paper, yes or no.**

## Tear-off and what's underneath

**1. Full tear-off to the deck.** Whether the old shingles come off or the new ones go over the top. Going over is cheaper today, hides anything wrong with the wood, and leaves two layers for the next person to remove. Ask how many layers are up there now.

**2. Rotted decking — an allowance in writing.** Nobody can see under a roof until it is open. You want a number of sheets included and a per-sheet price after that. "Priced as needed" is a blank check signed on a day when the roof is already off.

**3. Re-nailing the deck to code.** Many jurisdictions require the existing deck re-nailed before the new roof goes on. If it is not in the bid it becomes a change order, and it will be on the permit either way.

## The layers that keep water out

**4. Underlayment — synthetic or felt.** These are different products at different prices. A quote that just says "underlayment" has told you nothing. Ask for brand and product name.

**5. Ice-and-water shield.** The self-adhering membrane that protects valleys, eaves and every penetration. This is what saves you in wind-driven rain, and it is easy to leave off a bid without anyone noticing. Ask specifically where it is going.

**6. How the valleys are done.** Open metal or closed-cut are different work, different cost, different lifespan. If metal, ask the gauge.

**7. New flashing — not re-used.** Step flashing, counter flashing, chimney flashing. Where the roof meets a wall is the single most common place a new roof leaks, and re-using twenty-year-old metal on a new roof is how it happens.

**8. New pipe boots and vent collars.** The rubber collars around plumbing vents crack and fail. Old ones under a new roof will leak before the warranty is up.

**9. New drip edge.** Required by code in most places. The edge is where a roof starts to fail.

## The roof itself

**10. The shingle — brand AND exact product line.** "Architectural shingle" is a category, not a product. Two lines from the same manufacturer can differ by thousands across a roof and by decades of rated life. You want the product name.

**11. Manufacturer starter strip.** Cutting up field shingles to make starter along the eaves is faster and cheaper, and it can void the wind warranty.

**12. Manufacturer ridge cap.** Same issue at the ridge. Cut-up field shingles are not the cap product the warranty assumes.

**13. Ventilation — intake and exhaust.** Exhaust without enough intake at the soffits does nothing and bakes the roof. This is also one of the most common reasons a manufacturer denies a warranty claim.

## Paperwork, warranty and who is liable

**14. The permit, pulled by the contractor in their name.** If you pull it yourself, the county treats you as the contractor, and you own every defect. Confirm the fee is in the price.

**15. License number on the quote.** You can verify it in two minutes for free. A quote without one is not a quote from a licensed contractor.

**16. Liability and workers' compensation certificates.** Ask for both, sent directly by the insurer with your address listed. If someone is injured on your roof and there is no comp coverage, the claim can find its way to your homeowner's policy.

**17. Which manufacturer warranty you actually get.** The headline warranty usually requires a certified installer and a registration filed after the job. Ask who registers it and what you must do to keep it valid.

**18. Workmanship warranty — years, in writing, transferable.** The manufacturer covers the shingle. Only the contractor covers the installation, and installation is what leaks. Ask whether it transfers if you sell.

## Money and the day itself

**19. Payment schedule.** Deposit, draws, and what the final payment is tied to. A large deposit before any material has been delivered is the warning sign in this trade.

**20. Change orders priced and signed before the work.** This is the single clause that prevents a surprise invoice after the roof is already open.

**21. Start window and duration.** How soon, how many days, and what happens if weather stops the job mid-way with your roof open.

**22. Property protection and cleanup.** Landscaping, pool cage, AC unit, and a magnet sweep for nails at the end. Nails in the driveway and a damaged condenser are the two most common complaints after a re-roof.

## How to use this

Take your quotes and mark each line "in writing", "excluded", or "not addressed". Most quotes will come out with ten or more lines unaddressed, which is normal — it tells you what to ask, not that the contractor is bad.

Then ask. Write the answers down. Ask each contractor to re-issue the quote with those answers included, and compare the new numbers. A contractor who is happy to put it in writing has told you something about how the job will go. So has one who is not.

A quote that covers all twenty-two lines and comes in higher is not the expensive quote. It is the only one you can actually hold someone to.
