Every roofer says referrals are their best source of work. Very few have a referral program that does anything. The card that says "we love referrals" gets left on the kitchen counter, the reward is paid late or not at all, and nobody knows which jobs came from where. This is how to build one that produces signed contracts, and the one Florida rule you have to design around.

## Start with your own numbers

Before you design anything, pull your last 30 closed jobs and write the source next to each one: paid lead, door knock, sign, past customer, neighbor of a job, online. Most companies that do this find two things. Referred and neighbor jobs close at a far higher rate than paid leads, and they are the source nobody spends any money or time on.

That exercise tells you what a referral is worth to you, which tells you what you can afford to pay for one.

## Why most programs fail

- **One ask, at the wrong time.** The line about referrals is on the last page of the proposal, read on the day the homeowner is most worried about price.
- **The reward comes late.** A check that shows up three months later, after the referrer has to remind you, teaches people not to bother.
- **Nothing is tracked.** If you cannot say who sent whom, you cannot pay reliably, and you cannot tell whether the program works.
- **It relies on a card.** Cards get lost. A link gets forwarded.

## Ask at the three right moments

The best time to ask is when the homeowner is happiest with you, which is almost never the day they sign.

1. **The final walk-through.** The roof is done, the yard is clean, they are standing in the driveway looking up. Ask then, in person, and hand them the link.
2. **After the first hard rain.** Two or three weeks later, a short check-in: "How did it hold up in that storm Tuesday?" They say fine. You say thanks, and remind them the referral link is in their portal.
3. **When the next storm is in the news.** Every hurricane cone and every hail event sends neighbors up ladders and onto Google. Your past customers are being asked "who did your roof?" that week. Make sure they have something to forward.

## Work the street while you are on it

Most of your future referrals live within sight of the job you are doing today.

- Put the yard sign up the morning of tear-off, not the day you finish. A sign on a bare deck says "this is who is doing the work." A sign on a finished roof says "this is who did it," and half the neighbors have already stopped looking.
- Walk a short letter to the five or six nearest houses the day you start: who you are, that you will be on the street for a few days, that the crew will keep the site clean, and your number. Tuck a photo of a finished roof in the envelope.
- Keep the site clean every single day. The magnet sweep and the tarps over the landscaping are the ad. Neighbors judge a roofer by what the driveway looks like at 5 p.m.
- After the final inspection, with the homeowner's permission, send a follow-up to the same houses with a photo of the finished roof. "Your neighbor at 412 just had this done. Here is what it looked like." That letter closes.

## Design the reward so it gets used

- **Pay on the signed contract, not the lead.** You are rewarding a job, not a phone call. Say so plainly so nobody feels cheated.
- **Pick a number you can pay fast.** Many roofers land between $250 and $500 on a retail re-roof. Whatever the number, keep it under a couple of percent of your average job so you never hesitate to pay it.
- **Pay within a week of signing.** Speed is the whole reputation of the program. Send the reward with a note, and the referrer will do it again.
- **Give the new customer something too.** A small credit for the person who was referred makes the referrer feel like they handed a friend a favor, not a sales pitch.
- **Make it tiered if you want a few champions.** A second referral pays more. The handful of people who love talking about their roof will carry the program.

## Make it a link, not a card

A referral program that runs on a link solves the tracking problem by itself. The homeowner gets their own link in their portal. They forward it by text. The person who opens it lands on a page that already knows who sent them. You know who to pay, the referrer knows they will be paid, and you can count referrals by month without asking anyone.

Whatever tool you use, the rule is the same: the referral has to be something a homeowner can send from their phone in ten seconds.

## The Florida rule you must design around

Florida Statute 489.147 makes it illegal for a roofing contractor to offer anything of value to a residential property owner to induce them to file an insurance claim, or to let you inspect the roof for the purpose of making a claim. That covers referral rewards.

The safe design is simple. Tie referral rewards to retail work, meaning cash or financed re-roofs, and never to an insurance claim. Do not pay a referral bonus for a claim job. Do not advertise the program in a way that suggests a homeowner gets paid if their neighbor files a claim. Read the statute yourself and keep a copy of your program terms with your contracts.

## Track it or it did not happen

Put a source field on every lead and make it required. Count referral leads and referral closes every month. If the number is flat, the program is not being asked for, and you go back to the three moments above. If it is growing, raise the reward and put more effort into the street letters.

## Where a customer portal fits

A referral link the homeowner already has, in a portal they already open to check progress and pay draws, gets used far more than a card in a drawer. The portal shows them the invite, tracks who they sent it to, and shows the reward when it unlocks. The ask happens without anybody in your office remembering to make it.
