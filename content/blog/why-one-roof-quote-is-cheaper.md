You have three quotes for the same roof. One is $11,400, one is $14,200, one is $16,900. Same house, same square footage, three numbers that are almost $5,500 apart. The natural conclusion is that somebody is overcharging you.

Usually nobody is. The three contractors are not pricing the same job.

A roof quote is one number sitting on top of roughly twenty decisions, and most quotes only write down a few of them. The contractor who decided to replace all the flashing, include an allowance for rotted wood, and use the manufacturer's starter strip has a higher number than the contractor who left all three unaddressed. He is not more expensive. He is more specific.

## The number you are comparing is not the scope

Here is the same three quotes with the scope filled in:

| | Quote A — $11,400 | Quote B — $16,900 | Quote C — $14,200 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tear-off | Not stated | Full, to the deck | Full, to the deck |
| Rotted decking | No allowance | 3 sheets included, $95/sheet after | "Priced as needed" |
| Flashing | Re-used | All new | All new |
| Shingle | "Architectural" | Named line, warranty registered | Named line |
| Ventilation | Not mentioned | Ridge and intake | Not addressed |
| Permit | Not mentioned | Pulled by contractor | Homeowner pulls |
| Workmanship warranty | Not mentioned | 10 years, transferable | 2 years |

Quote A is not a $5,500 saving. It is a quote with six unanswered questions, and every one of them is a place where money arrives later or quality leaves quietly.

## The four gaps that move the price most

**Tear-off versus going over the top.** Laying new shingles over the old roof is faster and cheaper today. It also means nobody looked at the wood, the new roof carries the old roof's defects, and you have two layers to pay to remove next time. Some roofs can be legitimately layered; the point is that you should know which you are buying.

**The decking allowance.** Nobody knows what is under a roof until it is open. A quote that says nothing about rotted plywood is not a quote that found none — it is a quote that will price it on the day, when the roof is off, the crew is on site, and you have no leverage at all. "Three sheets included, $95 per sheet after that" is a real answer. "Priced as needed" is not.

**Flashing: new or re-used.** The metal where your roof meets a wall, a chimney, or a skylight is the most common place a new roof leaks. Re-using it saves a few hundred dollars and is invisible from the ground on the day the job finishes.

**What the warranty actually is.** The long manufacturer warranty on the brochure usually requires a certified installer and a registration filed after the job. Without both, you have the basic one. And the manufacturer only covers the shingle — if the roof leaks because of how it was installed, that is the contractor's workmanship warranty, which is a separate promise that some quotes never make.

## Why the cheapest quote is usually the shortest

This is not a rule about character. It is a rule about documents.

Writing "all new step and counter flashing, 26-gauge" into a quote commits a contractor to doing it and to paying for it. Leaving the line out costs nothing today and keeps the number low enough to win. A contractor who intends to do the work properly and writes it down will always look more expensive than one who intends the same thing but writes nothing — and identical to one who never intended it at all.

Which is exactly the problem. From the outside, a thorough contractor and an optimistic one produce the same-looking quote until you ask.

## What to do instead of comparing prices

Get the three quotes onto the same spec sheet. Go line by line — tear-off, decking allowance, underlayment, ice-and-water shield, flashing, shingle line, starter, ridge cap, ventilation, permit, warranties, payment schedule — and for each quote mark what is **in writing**. Not what was said in the driveway. What is on the paper.

You will end up with two things: a short list of lines where the quotes genuinely disagree, which is your answer to where the money went, and a list of questions for each contractor. Ask them, write down the answers, then have each contractor re-issue the quote with the answers in it.

Now the numbers mean something. If the cheap quote comes back at $15,800 once it covers the same work, you learned something real. If it comes back unchanged and the contractor is annoyed you asked, you learned something more useful.

## One thing that costs nothing

Look up every license number before you do any of this. It takes two minutes, it is free, and a quote without a license number on it is not a quote from a licensed contractor. Ask for the general liability and workers' compensation certificates to be sent to you directly by the insurer, not forwarded by the contractor.
